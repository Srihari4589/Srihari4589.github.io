Background

The Foundation of Modern Banking

Credit risk sits at the foundation of every lending decision made by banks, credit card companies, and financial institutions worldwide. Before extending a loan or approving a credit card, lenders must assess whether a borrower is genuinely capable of repaying what they owe — a question that carries enormous consequences for both parties. When credit risk is poorly evaluated, borrowers take on debt they cannot manage, and lenders absorb losses that destabilize entire financial systems. The key to accurate credit risk assessment lies not in a single data point, but in the patterns hidden within a borrower's financial history. Credit and debit transaction history reveals far more than a simple balance — the ratio of credits to debits, the frequency of transactions, and the consistency of deposits all paint a detailed picture of financial behavior. A borrower who maintains steady, growing monthly deposits signals very different risk than one who makes large, irregular transactions with no predictable pattern. The growth trend of account balances over time is particularly telling — consistent monthly growth demonstrates financial discipline that static snapshots cannot capture. Beyond personal financial data, a borrower's occupation and business activities provide critical context for estimating repayment capacity. Banks occupy a uniquely powerful position in this process, holding access to market-level data that allows risk prediction far beyond what any individual credit score could reflect. The combination of transactional patterns, behavioral signals, and economic context creates a far richer picture of creditworthiness than any single metric. When all these factors are analyzed together, a more accurate and fair assessment of credit risk emerges — one that protects both the lender and the borrower.

Current Methods & Their Limitations

How Banks Currently Handle Credit Risk

The most widely used credit risk tool in existence today is the FICO score — a model built on just five variables: payment history, amounts owed, length of credit history, new credit inquiries, and credit mix. While the FICO score represented a significant advancement when introduced in 1989, it was designed for a world where data was scarce and computation was expensive. The fundamental weakness of the FICO model is that it reduces the entire complexity of a person's financial life into a single number derived from five narrow inputs. An estimated 26 million Americans are considered "credit invisible" — they lack sufficient credit history to receive a FICO score at all — and are locked out of formal lending markets as a result. Millions more receive inaccurate scores that either deny them credit they could responsibly manage or approve them for amounts beyond their repayment capacity. The consequences of these failures are not abstract — in 2008, systemic failures in credit risk evaluation triggered the worst global financial crisis since the Great Depression. The crisis wiped out an estimated $10 trillion in household wealth and caused institutions like Lehman Brothers and Washington Mutual to collapse entirely. The FICO model fails to capture behavioral signals that real-world data makes visible — transaction frequency, deposit growth trends, and borrower occupation are all ignored. For banks holding vast amounts of transactional and market data, relying on five variables to make trillion-dollar lending decisions represents a massive missed opportunity. The 2008 crisis demonstrated that poor credit risk models do not just hurt individual borrowers — they can bring down entire economies. A better approach demands a fundamentally different kind of tool — one that processes many variables simultaneously and learns from new data as economic conditions change.

Who It Affects

The Human Cost of Credit Risk

Credit risk does not affect only banks and financial institutions — its consequences ripple through every layer of society, touching individuals, families, small businesses, and entire national economies. For the average person, access to affordable credit determines whether a home can be purchased, a car can be financed, or a small business can be launched. When credit risk is assessed poorly, deserving borrowers are denied opportunities that could transform their lives, while undeserving borrowers receive loans they cannot repay — creating a cycle of debt that is difficult to escape. Small business owners are particularly vulnerable to flawed credit assessment, as access to capital is often the single most important factor in whether a new business survives its first five years. In the aftermath of the 2008 financial crisis, governments and regulators around the world recognized that the existing credit risk framework had failed catastrophically, and began implementing sweeping reforms. The Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010 introduced the most significant overhaul of financial regulation in the United States since the Great Depression, establishing new oversight bodies and stricter lending standards. Consumer advocacy groups and civil rights organizations have long argued that traditional credit scoring systems systematically disadvantage minority communities, immigrants, and low-income households who lack access to formal banking. International efforts to expand financial inclusion — the goal of bringing unbanked and underbanked populations into the formal financial system — have gained significant momentum in the past decade. Mobile banking and fintech innovation have opened new pathways for assessing creditworthiness in developing economies, using alternative data sources such as mobile payment histories and utility records. Despite these advances, billions of people worldwide remain excluded from formal credit markets, unable to access the capital needed to improve their economic circumstances. The challenge of building a credit system that is simultaneously accurate, fair, and inclusive remains one of the most important unsolved problems in modern finance.

Module 2 Project

Clustering

Applying K-Means and Hierarchical clustering to identify natural borrower segments in the credit risk dataset and discover which groups are most associated with loan default.

Overview

What Is Clustering?

Clustering is an unsupervised machine learning technique that groups data points together based on similarity — without being told in advance what the groups should be. Unlike supervised learning, clustering discovers natural patterns in data on its own. In the context of credit risk, clustering can reveal natural segments of borrowers that share similar financial profiles, helping lenders understand who their customers really are beyond simple credit scores.

Two main approaches are used in this analysis. The first is K-Means clustering — a partitional method that divides borrowers into a fixed number of groups (k) by minimizing the distance between each borrower and the center of their assigned cluster. K-Means is fast, scalable, and works well on large datasets like the Lending Club data. The second approach is Hierarchical clustering — specifically agglomerative clustering — which builds a tree of clusters from the bottom up by merging the most similar borrowers step by step. The result is a dendrogram that visually shows how clusters relate to each other at different levels of similarity.

For the hierarchical clustering, Cosine Similarity is used as the distance measure. Cosine similarity measures the angle between two data vectors — making it effective for detecting borrowers who have proportionally similar financial profiles even if their absolute values differ. The combination of K-Means and hierarchical clustering provides both a scalable segmentation and a detailed visual understanding of borrower groupings in the credit risk dataset.

K-Means Clustering

Partitional method — divides borrowers into k groups by minimizing within-cluster distance. Fast and scalable for large datasets. Best k selected using the Silhouette method.

Hierarchical Clustering

Agglomerative method — builds a tree of clusters bottom-up. Uses Cosine Similarity as distance measure. Results visualized as a dendrogram showing cluster relationships.

Goal

Identify natural borrower segments and determine which clusters have the highest default rates — providing actionable insight for credit risk assessment.

Conclusions

What Clustering Revealed About Credit Risk

Clustering analysis of Lending Club borrower data revealed that four natural groupings exist within the lending population — groupings that align strongly with default risk. Cluster 2 emerged as the lowest-risk segment with only a 9.9% default rate, likely representing borrowers with strong credit histories, stable incomes, and low debt burdens. Cluster 3 showed the highest default rate at 26.1%, more than double that of Cluster 2, suggesting a clearly distinguishable high-risk borrower profile. The K-Means scatter plot confirmed that FICO score and interest rate are strong separators between clusters — high-FICO borrowers consistently receive lower interest rates and default less frequently. The hierarchical dendrogram revealed that borrower risk exists on a spectrum, with gradual transitions between low-risk and high-risk profiles rather than hard boundaries. These findings suggest that simple unsupervised clustering — without any labeled outcomes — can effectively identify meaningful borrower risk segments that could serve as an early warning system for lenders.

Module 2 — Dimensionality Reduction

PCA — Principal Component Analysis

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is a dimensionality reduction technique that transforms high-dimensional data into a smaller set of uncorrelated variables called principal components, while preserving as much of the original variance as possible.

Overview

What is PCA?

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is a mathematical technique that reduces the number of variables in a dataset while retaining the most important patterns and relationships. High-dimensional data — data with many variables — is difficult to visualize and analyze directly. PCA solves this by finding new directions in the data (called principal components) that capture the maximum amount of variance. Each principal component is a linear combination of the original variables, ordered so that the first component captures the most variance, the second captures the next most, and so on.

The key mathematical concepts underlying PCA are eigenvalues and eigenvectors. Eigenvectors define the directions of the new feature space (the principal components), while eigenvalues indicate the amount of variance carried in each direction. By selecting only the top principal components — those with the largest eigenvalues — the dimensionality of the data can be dramatically reduced while preserving the essential structure. For credit risk data with 9 numeric features, PCA identifies which combinations of borrower characteristics explain the most variation across the lending population.

Dimensionality Reduction

Reduces 9 numeric borrower features to a smaller set of principal components that capture the most important patterns in the data.

Eigenvalues & Eigenvectors

Eigenvalues measure the variance explained by each component. Eigenvectors define the direction of each component in the original feature space.

Why PCA for Credit Risk?

PCA helps visualize high-dimensional borrower data, removes correlated features, and improves the performance of downstream clustering and classification models.

Figure 1. PCA finds the directions of maximum variance (PC1, PC2) and projects data onto fewer dimensions while retaining the most important information.

Data Preparation for PCA

PCA requires numeric data that has been standardized — each feature must have zero mean and unit variance so that no single variable dominates the principal components due to scale differences. The same 9 numeric features used for clustering were used for PCA: loan amount, interest rate, annual income, debt-to-income ratio, FICO score, revolving utilization, delinquencies, total accounts, and public records. Conclusions

What PCA Revealed About Credit Risk

PCA analysis of the 9 borrower features confirmed that credit risk data is genuinely multi-dimensional — no single variable dominates the variance, and five principal components are needed to explain 70% of the total information. PC1 (21.2% variance) appears most associated with credit quality indicators like FICO score and interest rate, while PC2 (18.4% variance) reflects borrower capacity variables like income and loan amount. The biplot confirmed that FICO score and interest rate point in opposite directions along PC1 — borrowers with high credit scores tend to receive lower interest rates. The four borrower clusters from K-Means analysis are visible but overlapping in the PC1-PC2 space, consistent with the moderate silhouette scores observed. This overlap confirms that credit risk is not cleanly separable along any two dimensions, reinforcing the value of using multiple machine learning methods to capture different aspects of the risk landscape.
